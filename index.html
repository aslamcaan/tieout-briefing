Someone usually types a company's accounts into a spreadsheet by hand. It takes hours, and a typo like 21,013,565 looks just like the right number, 21,031,565.

Built for the person doing the typing

The analyst or associate handed twenty companies on a Friday: private equity, corporate finance, credit, anyone screening private companies.

How it works

First, you name a company.

To show each step, we will follow one real company all the way through: Toyota Connected Europe, a UK company in the Toyota group. In the app you type any company's name or number.

Toyota Connected Europe LimitedCompany 11283634, active

tieout fetches its latest accounts from Companies House, the public register where every UK company files its accounts each year. For a US listed company it fetches the annual report (the 10-K) from the SEC instead.

Accounts to 31 Mar 2026full accounts, tagged

Then it reads every line.

Most accounts filed online carry hidden labels on each number, called tags, that say what the number is: this one is turnover, that one is cash. Where the tags are there, tieout reads them. Where they are not, it reads the page the way you would: the name on the left, the year columns on the right. If the page is only a scan, it first turns the picture into text (that step is called OCR).

Each line is then matched to a standard name, so "Turnover", "Revenue" and "Sales" all land on the same row of your spreadsheet.

Then it redoes every sum.

Accounts are full of totals that must add up: the lines under current assets must add to the total, and assets less liabilities must equal equity. tieout redoes every one. When a sum fails, it shows you where.

The gap of 2,173,221 is exactly the line "Debtors: due after more than one year", which has no row of its own in the standard layout. tieout does not force it into a row where it does not belong, and it does not hide it either: the one-pager reports the gap, so you know what is in the total.

Finally, every number gets a label.

You see at a glance which numbers a sum proved and which are worth a second look.

VERIFIEDRead from the filing and proved by a sum. Safe to use. It does not mean audited or true.
UNVERIFIEDRead from the filing, but no sum covers it. Usually right; worth a glance if it matters to you.
FLAGGEDA sum failed. You see the sum and the difference, and the number is kept out of the ratios.
n/dNot disclosed. The company does not report it. Never shown as zero.
missingThe filing shows the line, but tieout could not read it.

Where does AI come in?

Barely. If a PDF uses a line name tieout has never seen, a language model may suggest which standard line it means, and that is switched off by default. A model never reads a number, never adds anything up and never decides pass or fail. Code does all of that, so the same filing always gives the same answer.

How I kept the test fair

Locked away first

Before any tuning, companies were split into practice, tuning and test groups by a fixed rule on their company number. The test group stayed untouched while the reader was built.

Run once, on the record

The test group was only ever run through one logged command, and nothing was tuned on it afterwards. A second fresh set of 505 filings from October 2026, heavier on larger companies, came in at 0.5%.

Numbers filled by script

Every figure on this page is copied from the test result files by a script, not typed by hand, so the page cannot drift from what was measured.

Would it have caught Patisserie Valerie?

No.

In October 2018 the café chain announced potentially fraudulent accounting irregularities, including in its cash. tieout read its scanned accounts from the years before. In the last set, revenue was £114.2m (VERIFIED) and cash £21.5m (VERIFIED). Every sum passed.

That is the honest answer, and the important one. If false numbers are made to add up, arithmetic cannot tell them from true ones. VERIFIED means read correctly and adds up. It never means true.

What the sums did catch was tieout's own mistakes. When it misread the scale in one year's scan, the sums failed and it flagged those figures instead of using them.
